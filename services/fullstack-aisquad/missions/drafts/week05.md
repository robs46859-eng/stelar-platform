# Week 5: Days 29-35 Postpartum

## Body: Maternal Recovery & Breastfeeding/Pumping

By week 5 postpartum, your body continues to heal from pregnancy and delivery. Lochia (postpartum bleeding) should have transitioned to a yellowish-white color and be significantly lighter, often stopping completely by the end of this week for many people. If you had a cesarean birth, your incision should be well on its way to healing, though internal healing continues.

**Breastfeeding & Pumping**: Around this time, many mothers notice their milk supply regulating to match their baby's demand. The intense engorgement of early weeks has likely subsided, and breasts feel softer between feeds. However, this can sometimes trigger anxiety about supply—remember, softer breasts don't mean low supply; they mean your body has become efficient at producing exactly what your baby needs.

If you're pumping (whether for returning to work, building a freezer stash, or because baby isn't latching well), you may be establishing a pumping routine. Many working parents aim to pump about 1-1.5 ounces per hour separated from baby. Your pump parts should be inspected regularly—valves and membranes wear out and affect suction.

**Physical Recovery Tips**:
- Continue gentle exercises as approved by your provider—pelvic floor exercises (Kegels) and walking are excellent starting points
- Monitor your incision (if applicable) for any signs of infection: increasing redness, warmth, swelling, or pus
- If breastfeeding, ensure you're still taking prenatal vitamins and staying well-hydrated
- Pay attention to posture while feeding/pumping to prevent neck and shoulder tension
- Consider getting fitted for a supportive bra as your breasts may have changed size

## Baby: Newborn Development & Care

At 4-5 weeks old, your baby is becoming more alert and interactive. You may notice:
- Longer wake periods during the day (though still needing frequent naps)
- The emergence of social smiles (typically appearing between 6-8 weeks, but some babies start earlier)
- Improved head control during tummy time
- More coordinated movements as reflexes begin to integrate
- Possible beginning of more predictable patterns in feeding and sleep (though "predictable" is relative with newborns!)

**Sleep Development**: Your baby's sleep is still immature, but you might notice slightly longer stretches at night (perhaps 3-4 hours). They still need to feed frequently around the clock due to their small stomach capacity. Day/night confusion should be resolving as their circadian rhythm begins to develop.

**Feeding**: Breastfed babies typically nurse 8-12 times in 24 hours, though some may have slightly longer intervals now. If pumping, you might notice your baby taking larger volumes less frequently from a bottle compared to breastfeeding.

**Soothing Techniques**: As your baby becomes more aware of their surroundings, they may experience more overtiredness or overstimulation. Watch for early tired cues (yawning, eye rubs, fussiness) and aim to put them down for sleep before they become overtired.

## Mental Health: The Emotional Landscape

By week 5, the "baby blues" (if you experienced them) have likely resolved for most people, as hormonal shifts stabilize. However, this is also a time when postpartum depression (PPD) or anxiety (PPA) can emerge or become more noticeable if they were subtle earlier.

If you're experiencing persistent sadness, anxiety, irritability, or feelings of hopelessness that interfere with your ability to care for yourself or your baby, it's important to reach out for support. These feelings are not a reflection of your capability as a parent—they are treatable medical conditions.

**Mental Health Check-In**:
- Monitor your mood: Are you able to experience moments of joy or connection with your baby?
- Check your anxiety levels: Are worries about the baby consuming your thoughts most of the day?
- Assess your support system: Do you feel isolated or overwhelmed by responsibilities?
- Consider screening tools like the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS)—many OB providers offer this at postpartum visits

**Coping Strategies**:
- Prioritize rest whenever possible—even short periods of eyes-closed rest can help
- Accept help with specific tasks (someone to hold the baby while you shower, a friend bringing a meal)
- Connect with other parents—whether in person or through moderated online groups
- Practice self-compassion: treat yourself with the same kindness you'd offer a friend in your situation
- If feelings persist beyond 2 weeks, feel severe, or include thoughts of harm, contact your provider immediately

## Partner Playbook: How to Help in Week 5

Your partner's continued support remains vital as you navigate this stage. Here are concrete ways they can help:

- **Feeding Support**: If you're pumping, they can handle pump part washing and sterilizing, milk storage labeling, and bottle preparation. During breastfeeding, they can bring you water, snacks, and ensure you're comfortable.
- **Sleep Protection**: Help protect your sleep opportunities by taking on baby care during your designated rest periods—even if it's just holding a sleeping baby so you can nap lying down.
- **Household Management**: Continue to own meal preparation, laundry, and cleaning tasks. At this stage, you might be tempted to "do more," but protecting your recovery energy is crucial.
- **Emotional Check-Ins**: Regularly ask how you're *really* doing—not just "are you okay?" but "what's been challenging today?" and listen without trying to immediately fix things.
- **Baby Bonding**: Encourage skin-to-skin time, tummy time supervision, and talking/singing to the baby—these activities strengthen their bond while giving you breaks.
- **Mental Health Advocate**: If they notice signs of persistent anxiety or depression in you, gently encourage you to talk to your provider and offer to help make appointments or accompany you.

## Call Doctor? Red-Flag Table for Week 5

|| Symptom | Normal | When to Call |
||---------|--------|--------------|
|| **Bleeding (Lochia)** | Light yellow/white discharge, minimal amount, may be intermittent. Stopped completely by end of week for many. | Heavy bleeding (soaking more than one pad per hour), return to bright red bleeding after it had lightened, foul odor, fever/chills. |
|| **Perineal/C-Section Pain** | Mild soreness that continues to improve. Itching as healing progresses. | Increasing pain, pus, redness spreading from incision, fever, or pain that interferes with daily activities. |
|| **Breast Pain** | Softer breasts between feeds, occasional let-down sensation. Possible mild tenderness during pumps. | Cracked/bleeding nipples, flu-like symptoms (fever, chills, body aches), red streaks on breast (mastitis), hard painful lumps that don't improve with feeding/pumping. |
|| **Baby's Feeding** | Regular feeding cues, content after feeds, gaining weight appropriately. | Persistent lethargy, poor latch causing pain, projectile vomiting, fewer than 6 wet diapers/day, not gaining weight. |
|| **Emotional Health** | Mostly stable mood with normal new-parent worries. Ability to enjoy moments with baby. | Sadness/hopelessness lasting >2 weeks, inability to enjoy baby, anxiety/panic attacks, thoughts of self-harm or harming baby. |
|| **Fever** | None expected from normal postpartum recovery. | Temperature ≥100.4°F (38°C) — could indicate infection (uterine, mastitis, UTI, or incision infection). |
|| **Urination/Bowel Movements** | Regular urination without pain. Bowel movements varying from every few days to multiple times daily (especially if breastfeeding). | Inability to urinate, severe constipation with pain/blood, persistent diarrhea, or no BM by day 4-5 if previously regular. |

**Remember**: When in doubt, call your provider. It's always better to check—especially with symptoms like fever, persistent pain, or concerns about your mental health.
