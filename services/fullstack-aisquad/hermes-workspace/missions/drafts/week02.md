# Week 2: Days 8-14 Postpartum

## Body: Maternal Recovery & Breastfeeding Establishment

By week two, your body continues to heal while establishing a more predictable breastfeeding routine. Lochia (postpartum bleeding) should be lighter—pinkish-brown or yellowish-white—and decreasing in volume. Uterine cramping (afterpains) typically subsides as your uterus returns to near-pregnancy size. Perineal soreness from tearing or episiotomy should improve daily; if you have stitches, they’re likely dissolving or absorbed.

**Breastfeeding Establishment**: Around days 3-5, your milk “comes in,” transitioning from colostrum to mature milk. Your breasts may feel fuller, warmer, and leak between feeds—this is normal engorgement as supply regulates to demand. By week two, many mothers notice a more rhythmic feeding pattern, though cluster feeding (frequent feeds over a few hours) can still occur, especially in the evenings. Aim for 8-12 feeds per 24 hours, watching for baby’s hunger cues (rooting, sucking motions, hand-to-mouth) rather than the clock.

If you’re experiencing persistent nipple pain, cracking, or bleeding, assess latch: baby’s mouth should cover more of the areola below the nipple than above, with lips flanged outward. Consider consulting a lactation consultant if pain continues beyond initial sucks or if you see signs of mastitis (localized breast redness, warmth, fever, flu-like symptoms).

**Physical Recovery Tips**:
- Continue gentle walking; avoid heavy lifting (>10 lbs) and strenuous activity.
- If cleared by your provider, begin pelvic floor exercises (Kegels) to support healing.
- Monitor lochia: should be lightening; sudden return to bright red bleeding may indicate overexertion.
- Stay hydrated and prioritize protein-rich meals to support milk production and tissue repair.
- Nipple care: apply expressed breast milk or lanolin after feeds; let nipples air-dry to prevent cracking.

## Baby: Newborn Care & Feeding

At 1-2 weeks old, your baby is becoming more alert and responsive. You may notice longer awake periods where they study faces, track objects with their eyes, and begin to coordinate sucking, swallowing, and breathing during feeds. Sleep remains fragmented but may show early signs of patterns—some babies start to consolidate one longer stretch (though not yet “sleeping through the night”).

**Feeding Patterns**: Expect 8-12 feeds per day. Breastfed babies often exhibit cluster feeding, especially in the late afternoon/evening, which helps boost milk supply. Formula-fed babies may take 2-3 oz per feeding every 3-4 hours. Watch for satiety cues: relaxed hands, slowing sucking, turning away from breast/bottle.

**Weight Check**: Most babies regain birth weight by day 10-14. A temporary weight loss of up to 7-10% in the first few days is normal; steady gain thereafter indicates adequate feeding. Pediatricians typically check weight at the first-week visit.

**Skin & Cord Care**: 
- Umbilical cord stump usually falls off between days 7-14. Keep the area clean and dry; a few drops of blood when it separates are normal.
- Newborn acne or milia (tiny white bumps) may appear due to maternal hormones; these are harmless and resolve on their own.
- Continue sponge baths until the cord stump falls off and heals.

**Developmental Milestones**:
- Begins to recognize familiar voices and voices from in utero.
- Exhibits rooting and sucking reflexes strongly.
- May briefly lift head during tummy time (always supervised, awake).

## Mental Health: Settling Into the New Normal

Week two often brings a mix of relief and new challenges. The initial adrenaline rush of birth has faded, and the reality of round-the-clock care sets in. Baby blues—mood swings, tearfulness, irritability—typically peak around day 3-5 and should be improving by the end of week two. However, fatigue, hormonal fluctuations, and adjustment to new responsibilities can still leave you feeling overwhelmed.

If sadness, hopelessness, or anxiety persists beyond two weeks, feels severe, or interferes with your ability to care for yourself or your baby, consider screening for postpartum depression (PPD) or anxiety (PPA). Early intervention improves outcomes. Tools like the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS) can help identify symptoms; scores ≥10 warrant discussion with your provider.

**Coping Strategies**:
- Prioritize sleep when possible—nap when the baby naps, even if short.
- Accept help: delegate household tasks, meal prep, or older child care to partners, family, or friends.
- Connect with other new mothers via support groups (in-person or online) to normalize experiences.
- Practice self-compassion: remind yourself that healing and adjustment take time.
- Limit decision fatigue: simplify routines (e.g., outfit choices, meal planning) to conserve mental energy.

## Partner Playbook: How to Help in Week 2

As you settle into a rhythm, your partner’s support remains vital. Week two is a good time to establish routines that distribute nighttime responsibilities and prevent burnout.

- **Night Shifts**: If breastfeeding, partner can handle burping, diaper changes, and settling the baby after feeds so you can return to sleep faster. If bottle-feeding (expressed milk or formula), they can take full feeds.
- **Daytime Relief**: Partner can wear the baby in a carrier for walks or naps, giving you uninterrupted time to shower, nap, or eat.
- **Household Management**: Take ownership of meals, grocery shopping, laundry, and cleaning—especially tasks that require bending or lifting if you’re still healing.
- **Emotional Check-Ins**: Regularly ask, “How are you *really* feeling?” Listen without rushing to fix; validate their experience.
- **Baby Bonding**: Encourage skin-to-skin time (baby on bare chest) to soothe the baby and strengthen attachment. Partners can also try infant massage or simply talking/singing to the baby during awake times.
- **Self-Care Facilitation**: Encourage and protect time for the mother to engage in restorative activities—whether that’s a shower, a walk, or a hobby—for even 15-20 minutes.

## Call Doctor? Red-Flag Table for Week 2

|| Symptom | Normal | When to Call |
||---------|--------|--------------|
|| **Bleeding (Lochia)** | Light pink/brown/yellowish discharge, steadily decreasing. Soaks <1 pad per hour. | Sudden return to bright red bleeding, soaking >1 pad/hr, large clots, foul odor, fever/chills. |
|| **Perineal Pain** | Mild soreness, improving daily. Stitches dissolving/absorbed without complication. | Increasing pain, pus, red streaks, fever, pain interfering with sitting/urination/bowel movements. |
|| **Breast Pain** | Engorgement resolving; occasional fullness before feeds. Niple tenderness improving. | Persistent nipple pain/cracking, flu-like symptoms (fever >100.4°F), localized red/warm breast area (mastitis), red streaks from nipple. |
|| **Baby’s Jaundice** | Mild yellowing of face/eyes, improving with feeding. Peaks day 3-5, fading by week 2. | Yellowing spreading to abdomen/arms/legs, baby lethargic, poor feeding, jaundice worsening after day 5 or persisting beyond week 2. |
|| **Emotional Health** | Mood swings, irritability, tearfulness improving; baby blues resolving by end of week 2. | Sadness/hopelessness >2 weeks, inability to enjoy baby, anxiety/panic attacks, thoughts of self-harm/harm to baby, hallucinations/delusions. |
|| **Fever** | None expected; low-grade fever (<100.4°F) possible with engorgement. | Temperature ≥100.4°F (38°C) — could indicate infection (uterine, mastitis, UTI, incision). |
|| **Urination/Bowel Movements** | Regular urination; bowel movements may occur every 1-2 days (softer if breastfeeding). | Inability to urinate, severe constipation with pain/blood, no BM by day 4-5, burning urination. |
|| **Baby’s Weight/Feeding** | Regaining birth weight by day 10-14, steady gain thereafter. Content after feeds, 6+ wet diapers/day. | Not regaining birth weight by day 14, persistent lethargy, weak suck, fewer than 6 wet diapers/day after day 5, projectile vomiting. |

**Remember**: When in doubt, call your provider. It’s always better to check.
