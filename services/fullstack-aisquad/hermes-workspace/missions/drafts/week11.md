# Week 11: Days 71-77 Postpartum

## Body: Maternal Recovery & Breastfeeding/Pumping

By week 11, most lochia has resolved, and you may notice the return of your menstrual cycle—especially if you're not exclusively breastfeeding. Your body continues to heal from pregnancy and delivery, with pelvic floor muscles gradually regaining strength. If you had a cesarean section, the incision is fully healed, though occasional numbness or itching around the scar is normal.

**Breastfeeding & Pumping**: At this stage, many mothers establish a steady milk supply that matches their baby's demand. If you're pumping (for return to work, building a freezer stash, or feeding multiples), you may be pumping 2-3 times per day while breastfeeding on demand. It's normal for pump output to fluctuate based on time of day, hydration, and stress. Remember: pump output is not a perfect measure of milk supply—your baby is far more efficient at extracting milk than any pump.

**Physical Recovery Tips**:
- Continue pelvic floor exercises (Kegels) to improve urinary control and support pelvic organs.
- If you experience leakage when laughing, sneezing, or exercising, know that this is common but should improve with consistent pelvic floor therapy.
- Gentle strength training (bodyweight squats, glute bridges) can help restore muscle tone; avoid high-impact activities until cleared by your provider.
- Monitor your c-section or perineal scar for signs of infection (redness, swelling, pus, worsening pain).
- Stay hydrated and prioritize protein-rich foods to support tissue repair and milk production.
- Schedule your postpartum checkup if you haven't already (typically around 6 weeks, but many providers see patients up to 12 weeks).

## Baby: Newborn Development & Feeding

Your baby is now about 2.5–3 months old and becoming more interactive and predictable. They may start to settle into a semi-regular rhythm for sleep and feeds, though variations are still normal.

**Developmental Milestones**:
- Begins to push up onto elbows during tummy time and may lift head and chest.
- Opens and shuts hands, brings hands to mouth, and may start to swipe at dangling objects.
- Recognizes familiar faces and voices, and may smile in response to your smile (social smile).
- Coos and makes vowel-like sounds ("ah-goo", "oooh").
- Begins to follow moving objects with eyes and shows interest in complex patterns.

**Sleep**: Total sleep averages 14–16 hours per day, with longer nighttime stretches (possibly 5–6 hours) and 3–4 daytime naps. Sleep regressions can pop up around growth spurts or developmental leaps, but many babies are capable of self-soothing to some extent.

**Feeding**: Breastfed babies typically feed 6–8 times per day; formula-fed babies may take 4–5 bottles of 6–7 oz each. If you're breastfeeding, you might notice shorter, more efficient feeds as your baby becomes a pro at latching. Pumping mothers may store milk for occasional bottle feeds to allow partner involvement or personal time.

**Tummy Time**: Aim for short, frequent sessions (2–3 minutes, 2–3 times per day) to build neck, shoulder, and arm strength. Always supervise and place baby on a firm, safe surface.

## Mental Health: Navigating the Emotional Landscape

By week 11, the intense "baby blues" have usually resolved, but some mothers continue to experience anxiety, irritability, or low mood. It's important to distinguish between normal adjustment challenges and symptoms that may indicate postpartum depression (PPD) or anxiety (PPA), which can emerge anytime in the first year.

> 
> "I snap at my partner over small things and feel guilty afterward" — Signal from r/beyondthebump

**Check-In**:
- Do you feel able to enjoy moments with your baby, or does guilt or worry dominate?
- Are you withdrawing from friends, family, or activities you used to enjoy?
- Do you feel hopeless, worthless, or excessively guilty about your parenting?
- Are you experiencing panic attacks, racing thoughts, or constant worry about your baby's safety?

If you answered yes to several of these, or if symptoms interfere with daily functioning, consider reaching out to your provider, a therapist, or a postpartum support line. Effective treatments include therapy (CBT, interpersonal therapy), support groups, and, in some cases, medication deemed safe for breastfeeding.

**Coping Strategies**:
- Practice "good enough" parenting—perfection is not the goal.
- Schedule small pockets of time for yourself (even 10 minutes to shower, walk, or sip tea).
- Share your feelings with your partner or a trusted friend; verbalizing worries often reduces their power.
- Consider mindfulness or breathing exercises to manage acute anxiety.
- Continue taking prenatal vitamins if breastfeeding, as nutrient deficiencies can exacerbate mood symptoms.

## Partner Playbook: How to Help in Week 11

Your partner's role evolves as you both gain confidence. At this stage, practical support and emotional attunement are key.

- **Feeding Support**: If you're pumping, your partner can handle bottle feeds (preparing, feeding, cleaning), allowing you to rest or pump. If breastfeeding, they can burp the baby, change diapers, and bring you water/snacks during feeds.
- **Sleep Assistance**: Encourage your partner to take on a morning or evening feeding so you can get a longer block of uninterrupted sleep. Even one night shift per week can make a difference.
- **Household & Logistics**: Share responsibility for meal prep, grocery shopping, and cleaning. Consider a weekly "reset" where your partner handles chores while you focus on self-care or baby bonding.
- **Emotional Support**: Check in regularly: "How are you *really* feeling?" Listen without jumping to fix things. Validate their experience: "It sounds like you're feeling overwhelmed—that makes sense."
- **Baby Bonding**: Encourage skin-to-skin time (baby on partner's bare chest), which helps regulate the baby's temperature and strengthens their bond. Partners can also read, sing, or do tummy time with the baby.
- **Personal Time**: Help the mother grab guilt-free breaks—whether it's a solo walk, a coffee with a friend, or a short nap.

## Call Doctor? Red-Flag Table for Week 11

|| Symptom | Normal | When to Call |
|---|---------|--------|--------------|
| **Bleeding (Lochia)** | Should have resolved by now; occasional spotting if period returns. | Heavy bleeding (soaking >1 pad/hour), clots larger than a golf ball, foul odor, fever/chills. |
| **Perineal/C-Section Pain** | Mild discomfort or itching as tissues heal; scar may feel numb or tingly. | Increasing pain, redness, swelling, pus, separation of incision/c-section scar, fever. |
| **Breast Pain** | Occasional tenderness with let-down or missed pump/feed; normal breast fullness before feeds. | Cracked/bleeding nipples, flu-like symptoms (fever, chills, body aches), red streaks/warm area on breast (mastitis), lump that doesn't resolve after feeding/pumping. |
| **Baby's Feeding** | Regular wet/dirty diapers (6+ wet, 3+ dirty per day for breastfed); steady weight gain. | Fewer than 6 wet diapers/day, projectile vomiting, bloody stools, refusal to feed, lethargy, fever (≥100.4°F). |
| **Baby's Sleep** | Longer stretches at night, total 14–16 hrs/day; occasional night wakings. | Extreme difficulty waking baby for feeds, blue lips/fingertips, pauses in breathing >20 seconds, persistent inconsolable crying despite soothing. |
| **Emotional Health** | Mood fluctuations, occasional frustration or sadness, ability to feel joy. | Persistent sadness/hopelessness >2 weeks, inability to bond with baby, anxiety/panic attacks, thoughts of self-harm or harming baby, hearing/seeing things that aren't there. |
| **Urination/Bowel Movements** | Regular urination; bowel movements vary from 3x/day to 3x/week. | Pain/burning with urination, inability to urinate, severe constipation with pain/blood, no bowel movement for >5 days. |
| **Fever** | None expected from normal postpartum recovery. | Temperature ≥100.4°F (38°C) — could indicate infection (uterine, mastitis, UTI, incision). |

**Remember**: When in doubt, call your provider. It's always better to check.
