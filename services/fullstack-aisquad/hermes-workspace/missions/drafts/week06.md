# Week 6: Days 36-42 Postpartum

## Body: Maternal Recovery & Breastfeeding Maintenance

At six weeks postpartum, you're likely approaching your first postpartum checkup with your healthcare provider. Your body has made significant healing progress, but recovery continues. Lochia (postpartum bleeding) should have transitioned to a yellowish-white color and be much lighter or may have stopped completely. If you had a cesarean section, your incision should be well-healed, though you might still feel occasional tightness or itching.

**Breastfeeding Maintenance**: By week six, breastfeeding is often more established, but supply concerns can still arise. Many mothers experience a natural regulation of milk supply around this time, where breasts may feel less full between feeds. This doesn't necessarily mean low supply—it's your body adjusting to your baby's exact needs. Continue breastfeeding on demand, typically every 2-3 hours during the day with possibly longer stretches at night.

> "constantly told my milk wasn't enough which honestly left me pretty scarred and anxious about supply" — Signal from r/breastfeeding

If you're pumping, you might notice changes in output as your body regulates. Trust your baby's growth cues (weight gain, wet diapers, contentment after feeds) over pump output alone. If you're exclusively breastfeeding, you likely don't need to pump unless preparing for return to work or building a freezer stash.

**Physical Recovery Tips**:
- Schedule your 6-week postpartum checkup if you haven't already
- Continue gentle exercises like walking or postpartum yoga as cleared by your provider
- Pay attention to pelvic floor health—consider pelvic floor physical therapy if you experience urinary incontinence or pressure
- Maintain hydration and nutrition, especially if breastfeeding (aim for an extra 300-500 calories daily)
- Monitor your menstrual cycle return—some women ovulate before their first postpartum period

## Baby: Newborn Sleep, Feeding & Development

At six weeks, your baby is emerging from the newborn phase and showing more predictable patterns, though variability is still normal.

**Sleep**: Your baby may be beginning to consolidate sleep slightly, with perhaps one longer stretch at night (4-6 hours) though many still wake every 2-3 hours for feeds. Total sleep remains around 14-17 hours per day. Daytime naps are still short and irregular—this is normal newborn sleep biology, not a problem to fix.

> "sleep dumpster fire" — Signal from sleep-deprived parent

**Feeding**: Breastfed babies typically feed 7-9 times per day at this age. Growth spurts can happen around 6 weeks, leading to cluster feeding and increased fussiness for a few days. Follow your baby's cues rather than a strict schedule.

**Developmental Milestones**:
- Smiling responsively (social smiles) - likely emerging or well-established
- Beginning to coo and make vowel-like sounds
- Improved head control during tummy time
- Tracking objects and faces more smoothly
- May begin to discover hands and bring them to mouth

**Soothing**: The "witching hour" (evening fussiness) may peak around 6 weeks. Try different soothing techniques: swaddling, white noise, gentle rocking, pacifier, or skin-to-skin contact. Remember that crying is your baby's primary communication tool—it doesn't mean you're doing anything wrong.

## Mental Health: Navigating the 6-Week Mark

The six-week postpartum period often brings a mix of emotions. The initial "baby blues" should have resolved by now for most mothers. If you're still experiencing persistent sadness, anxiety, or irritability beyond two weeks, it's important to evaluate for postpartum depression or anxiety.

> "don't want to mess this up" — Signal from Quora

At your 6-week checkup, your provider should screen you for postpartum mood disorders using tools like the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS). Be honest about how you're feeling—these screenings work best when you answer truthfully.

**Mental Health Considerations at 6 Weeks**:
- It's normal to feel grief for your pre-baby life or identity shifts
- Relationship changes with your partner are common as you adjust to parenting
- Anxiety about returning to work (if applicable) may begin to surface
- Social isolation can creep in if you're not getting regular adult interaction

**Coping Strategies**:
- Continue attending postpartum support groups or mom meetups if helpful
- Prioritize sleep when possible—even short naps help cognitive function and mood regulation
- Practice self-compassion: you're learning a new role, and perfection isn't the goal
- Consider talk therapy if you're struggling—many therapists specialize in perinatal mental health
- Small daily pleasures matter: a hot shower, a favorite snack, five minutes of sunlight

## Partner Playbook: How to Help at Week 6

Your partner's role evolves as you both gain confidence in parenting. At six weeks, they can shift from primarily supporting your physical recovery to more active parenting and household balance.

> "Partner involvement is an underserved gap" — Signal insight from dossier

**Ways Your Partner Can Help**:
- **Night Time**: Take ownership of one night feeding (if bottle-feeding expressed milk) or bring baby to you for breastfeeding and handle burping/diaper changes so you can maximize sleep
- **Daytime Support**: Handle morning or evening routines so you can have predictable breaks for self-care, naps, or appointments
- **Household Management**: Take leadership on meal planning, grocery shopping, and maintaining basic household order—not just helping when asked
- **Baby Bonding**: Encourage dedicated one-on-one time through babywearing, tummy time supervision, or bath time
- **Emotional Labor**: Remember and initiate appointments, prescriptions, and baby supplies without needing reminders
- **Your Check-Ins**: Regularly ask not just "How are you?" but "What do you need from me today?" and actually listen to the answer

## Call Doctor? Red-Flag Table for Week 6

|| Symptom | Normal | When to Call |
|---------|--------|--------|--------------|
| **Bleeding (Lochia)** | Should be scant or stopped by 6 weeks; any remaining discharge is yellowish-white. | Heavy bleeding, return to bright red after lightening, large clots, foul odor. |
| **Perineal/C-Section Pain** | Mild discomfort possibly, but generally feeling significantly better. | Increasing pain, pus, redness, separation of incision/c-section wound, fever. |
| **Breast Pain** | Occasional fullness before feeds, resolving with breastfeeding/pumping. | Persistent pain, red streaks, flu-like symptoms (mastitis), cracked/bleeding nipples not improving with latch adjustments. |
| **Baby's Sleep** | 14-17 hours total/day with unpredictable patterns; may have one longer night stretch. | Extreme lethargy (difficult to wake for feeds), persistent inconsolable crying >3 hours/day, not gaining weight appropriately. |
| **Emotional Health** | Most baby blues resolved; typical adjustment emotions. | Sadness/hopelessness >2 weeks, anxiety interfering with function, panic attacks, thoughts of harming self/baby, inability to bond with baby. |
| **Fever** | None expected in either parent or baby. | Temperature ≥100.4°F (38°C) in you or baby—could indicate infection. |
| **Urination/Bowel Movements** | Regular pattern established; any incontinence improving. | Pain/burning urination, inability to urinate, no bowel movement by day 4-5 postpartum (if not resolved), blood in stool. |
| **Breastfeeding** | Established rhythm; supply regulating to baby's demand. | Baby consistently frustrated at breast, not gaining weight, fewer than 6 wet diapers/day, sudden drop in supply without explanation. |

**Remember**: Your 6-week postpartum checkup is an important milestone. Come prepared with questions about physical recovery, breastfeeding concerns, emotional health, and birth control options if desired. When in doubt between checkups, call your provider—it's always better to check.
